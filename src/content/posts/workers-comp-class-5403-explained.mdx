If you have W-2 employees on a framing crew, you carry workers' compensation — and the code used to rate that coverage has a bigger impact on your premium and your claims than almost any other detail on your policy. For wood framing and rough carpentry, that code is most often **NCCI class 5403 — Carpentry**. Here's what it means and why it matters.

## What class code 5403 covers

NCCI class 5403 applies to **carpentry operations**, including the wood framing, floor, wall, and roof work that residential and light-commercial framers do. It reflects a real, elevated injury exposure — falls from height, nail-gun and saw lacerations, struck-by injuries, and material-handling strains — and it's priced accordingly.

Some operations split out related work into separate codes: roof truss setting, siding, interior finish carpentry, or excavation each may carry their own classification. The point isn't to memorize the book — it's to make sure **your actual work is classified accurately**.

## How premium is calculated

Workers' comp premium for a given code is:

> **(Payroll ÷ 100) × class rate × experience modifier**

So a framing crew with $300,000 of 5403-coded payroll, a given rate, and a clean experience modifier produces a calculable premium. The variables that move it:

- **Payroll accuracy.** Underreporting payroll lowers your deposit but guarantees an audit bill later. Report it honestly.
- **The class rate.** This is set by the state and the classification; it's where correct coding pays off.
- **Your experience modifier (EMR).** A clean loss record pushes your modifier below 1.0 and saves you money year after year. A bad claims year pushes it above 1.0 and costs you.

## The two expensive mistakes

**Misclassification** is the first. Framers get lumped into a generic "construction" code that overcharges them, or into a low-exposure code that looks cheap — until a claim is investigated, the work is reclassified, and the carrier denies the claim and back-charges premium via audit.

**Misclassifying W-2 as 1099** is the second. Calling employees "subcontractors" to avoid premium doesn't hold up. If they're directed and controlled like employees, most states treat them as statutory employees — and you can be liable for both the unpaid premium and the claim.

## Why correct coding protects your claims

It's not just about price. If your crew is coded as something other than carpentry and a framer is injured in a fall, the carrier investigates whether the classification matches the actual work. A mismatch can delay — or jeopardize — the claim while the injured worker waits. Accurate classification keeps claims clean and fast.

## Fall protection is the lever that lowers 5403 costs

Because 5403 reflects fall and tool exposure, the single best way to control its cost is a **documented fall-protection and tool-safety program**. Underwriters and auditors reward crews that can show written safety procedures, training records, and consistent jobsite practice. Fewer falls means a better EMR — and a better EMR means a lower rate for years.
